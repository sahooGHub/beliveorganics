Chapter 01

Farming has many roots

Across places and generations, farmers have developed different ways to work with crops, animals, soil and water. Local practice varies; there is no single story that fits every region.

🌾

Crop diversity

Mixed cropping and rotations are approaches farmers use to grow more than one crop over time or in the same field. What works depends on the crops, local climate, soil, labour and market.

💧

Water knowledge

Traditional and modern water systems include tanks, wells, channels, bunds and irrigation. Their value depends on local geography, maintenance and how water is shared.

♻️

Returning nutrients

Compost, crop residues, manure and green manures are among practices used to return organic material to fields. Their effects depend on management and local conditions.

These are broad learning themes, not claims that historical farming was always sustainable or that traditional methods alone can meet every present-day need. We will add specific historical sources as individual claims are researched.

Chapter 02

The Green Revolution: gains and questions

India’s move toward high-yielding crop varieties and supporting inputs was shaped by food-security needs and public policy. Its effects differed by region and continue to be studied.

Why it matters to understand the context

Farming changes are made in response to real pressures, including food availability, farm income, water, technology and public policy. Looking back should include both the reasons a change was adopted and the outcomes people later observed.

Be.Live does not treat a complex period as a simple story of one solution being wholly good or bad. The questions are: who benefited, what costs emerged, where, and what evidence supports each conclusion?

What we need to source

Production trends, public programmes, groundwater changes, farmer economics and soil conditions need specific sources and dates. National averages do not describe every state, district or farm.

We are reviewing these topics and will add direct references before publishing numerical claims.

Chapter 03

Ask about trade-offs

Every farming approach involves choices. A fair comparison looks at more than yield: it includes costs, labour, water, soil, risk, food quality and whether there is a buyer.

🧾

Farm economics

What are the setup and ongoing costs? How much labour and skill are needed? What happens to income if yields, prices or input costs change?

🌧️

Land and water

Does the method suit the soil, rainfall, water access and climate risks at this particular site? Local measurement matters more than a generic label.

🛒

Market and trust

Who wants the produce, how reliably, at what price, and how will claims about growing practices be checked? Consumer awareness can help reveal demand, but must be connected to a workable route to market.

Chapter 04

A spectrum of farming methods

Labels such as conventional, organic, natural, regenerative and biodynamic cover varied practices. Compare the actual farm system and evidence, not just the label.

🚜

Conventional systems

May use improved varieties, synthetic fertilisers or crop-protection products, machinery and irrigation. The combination differs across farms and crops.

🌿

Certified organic

Certification means following a defined standard and inspection process. Rules differ by market and certification scheme; “organic” should not be used as a claim of specific health outcomes without evidence.

🐄

Natural farming approaches

Some approaches emphasize farm-made inputs, mulching and reduced reliance on purchased inputs. Results and costs are context-specific and should be assessed locally.

Source: Andhra Pradesh Community Managed Natural Farming (programme information)
🌳

Permaculture and agroforestry

These design approaches consider relationships among plants, animals, people, water and land. The design has to fit the site and the needs of the people managing it.

♻️

Regenerative agriculture

The term is used for a range of practices intended to improve ecological or soil outcomes. Ask which practices, which measurements, over what period and according to whose evidence.

🌙

Biodynamic agriculture

A farming approach with its own practices and certification systems. Descriptions of the approach should be distinguished from evidence for particular outcomes.

Chapter 05

Multi-layer farming: a design question

Can crops with different heights, root depths and growing periods share a site productively? It is a useful question to explore—not a guaranteed yield formula.

Above the canopyTall trees

Possible roles: shade, fruit, wind protection or habitat. Species selection and spacing depend on the site.

Tree layerSmall trees and palms

Consider light needs, mature canopy size, water use and harvest access.

Shrub layerPerennial shrubs

Candidate crops should fit local climate, shade and market demand.

Herb layerSeasonal crops and herbs

Plan around seasonal light, labour, pests and harvest windows.

Ground layerGround cover

Ground cover may protect soil, but crop choice and management matter.

Below groundRoots and tubers

Root depth varies by species and soil. Soil profile and water availability need checking.

Vertical spaceClimbers

Climbers may use supports or other plants; compatibility and competition need testing.

Be.Live learning note: A diagram of layers is a way to ask questions about space and roots. It does not show that every layer can be combined on every farm, or establish a yield advantage. We would need crop-specific advice, local trials and records of costs and harvest.

Chapter 07

Connect farms with people and markets

Rural production and urban demand can be connected in different ways. Each model needs a local buyer, fair terms, reliable logistics and farmer participation.

🌾

Questions for a rural growing base

  • What do farmers want to grow and what support do they need?
  • What are the local soil, water and climate conditions?
  • What routes exist to storage, transport and buyers?
  • How can farmers share knowledge and negotiate fair terms?
🏙️

Questions for consumer markets

  • Which people want the products and why?
  • What evidence would help them trust how food was grown?
  • What price and delivery options are realistic?
  • Can demand be dependable enough to guide production?
Possible bridge to explore: Community-supported agriculture and direct sales can connect consumers and producers, but they are not automatic solutions. Be.Live would need to validate demand, costs, delivery, risk-sharing and benefits with the people involved.

Chapter 08

Hydroponics and aquaponics

These systems grow plants without conventional field soil, using water-based nutrient delivery; aquaponics also integrates aquatic animals. Their suitability depends on crops, energy, water, skills, costs and markets.

💧

Hydroponics

Plants receive water and mineral nutrients in a managed system. System designs differ. Nutrient management, water quality, power reliability and operating skill are important considerations.

🐟

Aquaponics

Aquaponics combines fish or other aquatic animal production with plant growing in a managed water loop. The system needs careful monitoring and does not automatically use less water or cost less in every setting.

🔎

Questions before choosing

Which crops can be sold reliably? What are the setup and energy costs? How will water and nutrients be managed? What happens during a pump or power failure? Compare measured local data before making claims.
