The State of Our Soil:
Chemical NPK vs. Soil Biology

A research-led introduction to mineral fertilizers, organic inputs, and the living processes beneath our feet—without reducing a complex question to a false choice.

Be.Live Organics · Independent researchEvidence review · 12 min readPublished November 16, 2026

This is an educational summary, not agronomic advice.

Soil is not just a container for nutrients. It is a changing ecosystem of minerals, organic matter, water, roots, fungi, bacteria, and other organisms. Fertilizers influence that ecosystem—but their effects depend on how, how much, and where they are used.

The short answer

Mineral fertilizers and soil biology are not opposites. Plants need mineral nutrients, and soil organisms help transform and cycle nutrients; organic matter can feed organisms and influence water movement and soil structure. A fertilizer regime that supplies nutrients does not, by itself, describe the full condition of a soil.

Research reviews and long-term studies generally find that organic amendments can improve some biological and soil-organic-matter indicators, while mineral fertilizers can efficiently supply nutrients needed for crop growth. Effects vary by soil and management, and combined approaches are often studied because they address both crop nutrient needs and soil functions. This is not a universal prescription or a guarantee of higher yields.

First, the terms

NPK refers to fertilizers supplying the primary plant nutrients nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), and potassium (K). Mineral fertilizers can provide nutrients in forms plants can take up relatively quickly, but the nutrient source alone does not determine the effect: dose, timing, crop demand, soil pH, and losses to air or water matter.

Soil biology means the organisms living in soil and their interactions. Bacteria, fungi, earthworms, and many others participate in decomposition, nutrient transformations, aggregation, and other processes. Biological activity is not a stand-alone soil-health score, nor does more microbial diversity automatically mean better crop outcomes.

Organic amendments—such as compost, manure, or retained crop residues—add organic materials, and often nutrients. Their composition and nutrient release vary. “Organic” is not synonymous with risk-free, and inappropriate application can also cause nutrient excess or environmental harm.

What does the evidence say?

1. Nutrient supply and crop needs

Mineral fertilizers can make specific nutrients available in a predictable, concentrated form, which is useful when a crop has a diagnosed need. But a focus on N, P, and K alone may overlook other limiting factors, nutrient balances, or how much fertilizer a crop can actually use. Soil and crop testing, sound rates, and timing are important parts of responsible nutrient management.

2. Organic matter and soil organisms

Organic amendments supply carbon as well as nutrients, and studies report changes in soil organic matter and microbial communities under different fertilization regimes. The magnitude and direction depend on amendment type, climate, soil, crop, and duration. A review of fertilization research also emphasizes that microbial responses are complex; no single fertilizer category produces the same outcome everywhere.

3. Long-term soil function

Long-term comparisons are valuable because they track cumulative effects that short trials can miss. Recent work on long-term chemical and organic fertilization reports distinct effects on soil properties and microbial communities. Such results support looking beyond immediate yield—but they should not be generalized without checking the study’s crop, location, soil, and treatment details.

4. Integrated management is a question, not a shortcut

Combining mineral nutrients with organic inputs is one approach researchers investigate. It can potentially support crop nutrition while returning carbon and nutrients to soil, but the right balance is site-specific. Organic amendments need appropriate sourcing and nutrient accounting; mineral inputs need appropriate rates and application. The evidence supports careful comparison, not a blanket rule that one approach always wins.

What this means for a grower or learner

  • Start with the soil and crop context: soil tests, water conditions, crop history, and local advice.
  • Think beyond a bag label: consider nutrient balance, organic matter, cover, roots, rotations, and erosion.
  • Track outcomes over time: yields, input costs, soil indicators, and any signs of nutrient loss.
  • Treat organic and mineral inputs as materials with benefits, limits, and risks—not as moral categories.
  • Ask whether research conditions match the local field before adopting a practice.

Limits and open questions

This brief is a starting point, not a systematic review. The cited literature includes reviews and studies from different production systems, which cannot all be transferred directly to Odisha. Outcomes depend on soil type, rainfall and irrigation, crop sequence, input quality, rates, and years of management. Terms such as “soil health” and “microbial diversity” are measured in different ways across studies.

For Odisha, useful next steps include understanding local soil constraints and crop systems, identifying relevant long-term field research, and comparing practices with farmers and agricultural scientists. We welcome corrections and additional relevant studies.
