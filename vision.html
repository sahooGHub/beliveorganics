A work in progress, guided by honest questions

What are we trying to figure out?

Be.Live is exploring how stronger connections between food, farmers, knowledge and communities might be built. We are still learning. These are the questions we want to investigate—not claims that we have already solved them.

Our promise: We will distinguish what we know, what we have heard or read, what we are testing, and what remains uncertain.

The problem we see

Good pieces exist. The connections can be hard to find.

People may want trustworthy food but not know who grew it or how. Farmers may have practical knowledge but face challenges reaching customers, markets or useful support. Research, farm experience and consumer understanding often sit in separate places. Be.Live wants to learn whether it can help connect some of these pieces in a practical, locally relevant way.

Farmers↔Growing practices↔Food & trust↔Families & markets↔Knowledge shared back

Why hasn’t someone already done this?

Many people and organisations have already built parts of this picture—farms, farmer networks, organic brands, research, community-supported agriculture and awareness work. The challenge is not that nobody has tried. The question is whether these pieces can be connected in a way that works for a particular place and its farmers, customers and communities.

What might be different about Be.Live?

Our current idea is to connect learning, small practical experiments, farmer relationships, consumer awareness and open knowledge-sharing. That is an intention, not a proven advantage yet. We must learn from existing organisations and show, through practice, whether our approach adds value.

Who could benefit?

Start with people—and test the value for each.

Farmers

Potentially stronger customer relationships, shared learning and better access to markets. These benefits depend on whether the model improves income or reduces risk in practice.

Families and consumers

Potentially clearer information about how food is grown and more direct relationships with producers. Trust must be earned and claims must be verifiable.

Local communities

Potential opportunities to contribute skills, create local activity and strengthen connections with food and land—without assuming every community wants the same thing.

Learners and future collaborators

A place to share useful lessons from research, farm visits and experiments, including what did not work and why.

Place matters

Why explore Odisha, not Bengaluru’s outskirts?

We should compare actual locations and partnerships—not rely on broad assumptions about land prices or scale. Odisha is being considered as a place to learn with local communities and explore agricultural opportunities; Bengaluru may be valuable as a customer, knowledge and partner market.

Odisha: possible learning and growing base

Questions to investigate: local soil and water conditions, land access and tenure, infrastructure, skills, farmer interest, market routes, climate risks and community priorities. Access to Bhubaneswar and Cuttack may matter, but each proposed site needs its own assessment.

Bengaluru region: possible market and partnership base

Questions to investigate: customer demand, operating and land costs, competition, logistics, access to collaborators and whether proximity to consumers offsets higher costs. It is not simply “too expensive”; the trade-offs need evidence.

We should publish a location comparison only after gathering reliable local information. “Odisha is cheaper” or “Bengaluru is better” should not be presented as a fact without evidence for the specific sites and model.

Learning agenda

From podcasts and ideas to careful experiments.

Recent learning includes conversations and podcasts about polyhouse cultivation, multi-layer farming, crop combinations and how roots occupy different soil layers. We are also studying insights from practitioners such as Sagar Chauresia. These are prompts for further learning, not proof that a technique will work on a Be.Live site.

Multi-layer and companion planting

Which crop combinations can share light, space, water and nutrients? What are the labour, pest, harvest and market trade-offs?

Root layers and soil

How do candidate crops use different rooting depths? What do local soil tests, water availability and crop requirements tell us?

Polyhouse and shade-net systems

Which crops, seasons and buyers could justify the cost, energy, water, maintenance and skills required?

Other systems

Hydroponics, regenerative practices and community-supported models should be compared by purpose, costs, risks and local suitability—not treated as one-size-fits-all answers.

As the learning grows, we can document each topic with its source, key takeaway, local relevance, unanswered questions and next step. Technical claims and numerical figures should be checked against credible sources before publication.

Possibility, not a promise

How could we tell whether it is working?

We cannot responsibly give a success percentage today. The possibility depends on learning whether the approach is useful, affordable, wanted by farmers and customers, and able to handle real-world risks.

Study the place, people and existing work
Talk with farmers and potential customers
Run a small, measurable pilot
Share results, adjust, then decide whether to grow

Growth track

Our proposed sequence is learning → small demonstration → evaluate results → community participation → carefully developed partnerships or networks. Each next step should depend on evidence, capacity and local interest.

What “success” could include

Farmer economics and participation, customer trust and repeat demand, soil and water measures, reliable operations, knowledge shared and community priorities. Acres or revenue alone would not tell the whole story.

Business and funding are open questions

Build a model that supports the mission.

We have not settled on a business model or funding plan. Possible models to research—not decisions or promises—could include produce sales, subscriptions or community-supported agriculture, learning programmes, partnerships or grants. Each has different costs, responsibilities and risks.

Who might be customers?

Possible groups to talk with include households seeking traceable produce, local retailers, restaurants, institutions and community groups. We need to validate what they value, what they will pay for and what supply can reliably support.

How might it be funded?

Funding options to examine could include founder contributions, staged pilot budgets, grants, partnerships, customer pre-orders or investment if appropriate. Before seeking funds, define the pilot, budget, governance, risks and evidence of demand.

We should not imply that funding is secured, that a revenue stream is proven, or that investors will receive a particular return unless those facts are verified and formally approved.

Keep asking

This page should grow with what we learn.

As you watch more conversations, visit farms and study local conditions, we can add notes in plain language: what sparked the question, what the source said, what we think it might mean for Be.Live, what needs checking and what we plan to explore next.
